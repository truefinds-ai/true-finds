The four verdicts

Only four possible answers, each one deliberately simple. Two give you permission, one exposes the trick, one admits the limits.

GENUINE DEAL
A real low. Good time to buy.

The price is at or near this product's true low over its recorded history. Not "lower than the sticker," which any seller can arrange, but low against what it actually sold for.

INFLATED
Not a real discount. The 'was' price is not what this really sells for.

The discount is manufactured: the reference price is not what this product actually sells for, whether it was raised last week or has stood above the real price all along. Invisible without price history, obvious with it.

TYPICAL PRICE
Normal price. Fine to buy if you need it.

This is roughly what the product usually costs. Not a warning; if you need it today, the normal price is a fair price. Just the truth, so you decide with accurate expectations.

NOT ENOUGH DATA
Too new to judge. Not enough price history yet.

A tool that always has an answer is guessing some of the time. Our standard is the opposite: every verdict backed by data, or no verdict at all. When we don't know, we say so.

Why a verdict instead of a chart

Price history charts have existed for years, and most shoppers never use them, because reading a chart at the moment of purchase is work. You came to buy headphones, not to interpret a graph.

A verdict is the chart's conclusion with the work already done. The evidence stays available for anyone who wants to look deeper. But nobody has to earn the truth by studying a graph; it's just there, in plain words, next to the price it describes.

The privacy promise

Sinclair reads the product page you are on. Nothing else. No browsing history, no tracking across sites, and the narrowest permissions Chrome allows for the job.
